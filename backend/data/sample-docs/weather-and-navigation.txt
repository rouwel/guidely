Weather and Navigation in the Sound

Seasonal closure. The sound is officially closed to all but emergency and pilot
traffic between the first day of winter and the last day of the third winter
month, whenever recorded gusts at the North Pier exceed forty knots for a
continuous hour. The harbourmaster announces a closure by ringing the warning bell
three times and hoisting the black double flag above the harbour office. When a
closure is announced, all scheduled crossings are suspended until the harbourmaster
declares the sound open again, which requires gusts to fall below thirty knots and
remain there for two consecutive hours. Note that a closure does not require the
storm to have arrived: the warning bell is rung when the forecast meets the
threshold, not when the weather does, because the crossing takes hours and the
ferries cannot race a storm.

Beacons. The beacon lights of the sound - North Pier, Threnhold Point, and the
channel buoy - are extinguished for the winter and relit together at the spring
equinox, by custom on the same evening as the first festival of spring. The light
keepers are obliged to report any beacon that fails within a day of the failure,
and to repair or replace it within three days. In fog, the channel buoy rings a
bell driven by the swell rather than showing a light, and masters are expected to
use it as the primary mark of the approach.

Fog. When visibility falls below half a cable, masters of sailing vessels must
either rendezvous for a tow, anchor and wait, or heave to. Powered vessels may
continue, but only if they are sounding the fog horn at intervals of not more than
two minutes and have a lookout posted forward. The master remains free to choose;
yet whatever the choice, the master is answerable for it, and the rules of
pilotage and of the harbour apply just as strictly in fog as in a clear sky. Any
vessel that continues in fog without a horn and a lookout is in breach and may be
detained.

Master's discretion. Nothing in these rules removes the master's discretion to
decline a sailing or to turn back. The master is the sole judge of the vessel's
readiness, and a master who declines to sail against a gale warning is never to be
penalised for the refusal. This protection is absolute and was written into the
regulations on the recommendation of the inquiry into the Neverdine, whose
master's decision to sail in the face of a warning is still taught as the example
of what not to do.

Barometer. Every passenger vessel must carry a working barometer, checked before
each sailing and entered into the log. A falling barometer of more than eight
millibars in six hours is reportable to the harbour office before departure.
