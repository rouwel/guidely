The Sinking of the Neverdine

On the night of the 27th of Winter, the passenger ferry Neverdine left Harbourport
for the island of Threnhold on the last scheduled crossing of the season. She was
built forty years earlier for the coastal mail run and had been re-rated for
passenger service after a refit that the surveyors themselves described as
"adequate." She carried two hundred and forty-one souls: two hundred and fourteen
passengers and a crew of twenty-seven under Captain Maren Duse.

The storm that night was not unusual for the season, but it arrived faster than
the harbour office had predicted. By the time the Neverdine cleared the breakwater
the wind was gusting past forty knots out of the northwest and the sound was
running a heavy, short sea. Captain Duse had been offered the chance to delay the
sailing by the harbourmaster, who later testified that she shook her head and said
the cargo was already lashed and the passengers had booked to travel home that
night.

Witnesses from the shore reported the ferry listing steadily to starboard as she
approached the channel buoy. The tipping point appears to have been a cargo of
casks and iron bar stock loaded onto the upper deck against the recommendation of
the load plan. In heavy seas the cargo shifted, the starboard scuppers foundered,
and within minutes the Neverdine had rolled far enough that water began to take
the decks. She went over at the mooring approaches, less than a mile from the
beacon, in water the inquiry later found to be no deeper than the draught of her
own hull.

The rescue effort was swift and well drilled. Longboats from Threnhold and the
harbour tender pulled ninety-eight survivors from the water and from the upturned
hull, which floated for two hours before going down. One hundred and forty-three
people did not survive. Among them were Captain Duse, who stayed in the wheelhouse
throughout, and her first mate, who died clearing the port lifeboat when its falls
jammed.

The Board of Inquiry sat for six weeks. Its findings were recorded in three parts.
First, the load exceeded the upper-deck rating and had been shifted without a
revised stability calculation. Second, the hull had not been inspected for
corrosion in the nine years since the refit, and the plates around the waterline
were later found to be pitted to half their original thickness. Third, the vessel
sailed into a storm warning that the master had acknowledged in writing the same
afternoon. The inquiry concluded that the sinking was the result of accumulated
negligence rather than a single failure, and it made seven recommendations to the
harbour authority, including annual hull surveys, a hard load-plan review before
every seasonal crossing, and the requirement that masters file a written sea
worthiness declaration whenever a storm warning is in force for the sound.

The Neverdine herself was raised the following spring and broken up at the
Harbourport yard. A permanent memorial stands at the head of the North Pier, and
the day after the inquiry's report was published, the harbour authority enacted
the fourteen-day claims rule for bereaved families and owners of lost cargo, and
the new safety regulations for all vessels sailing the sound in the winter
months.
